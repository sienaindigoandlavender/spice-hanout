Ask three spice sellers in the medina for ras el hanout and you will get three different powders, three different colours and three speeches about why the other two are wrong.

The name means "head of the shop": the best the merchant has, mixed into one jar. There is no fixed recipe. Some count a dozen spices, some boast of thirty or more. Rose buds, cardamom, mace and long pepper go in when the seller wants to impress, cheaper fillers when he does not. The blend is a portrait of the shop.

That freedom is the fun of it. Ras el hanout goes into lamb with prunes, into a winter stew, rubbed onto a chicken before it roasts, a pinch into the couscous broth. Its job is to make a dish taste like it took all day, even when it did not.

Ours is built around cumin, coriander and ginger, lifted with cardamom, rose and lavender. It is our head of the shop. Try [[ras-el-hanout|it]] in the [[lamb-and-prune-tagine]] and tell us we are wrong.
