The host has the pot in one hand and a glass in the other, and he is in no hurry at all. A pinch of green pellets went in first, rinsed with a splash of boiling water that he swirled and threw away. Then a fist of mint, stalks and all, then sugar knocked off a white cone with a little hammer, then the water. Now he pours a glass, looks at it, and tips it straight back into the pot. He does it again. On the third go he lifts the pot as high as his arm will reach and the tea falls in a long thin rope, landing in the glass with a cap of foam. Only then is it ready.

This is atay, and in Morocco it is poured for everyone: the neighbour who came to borrow a pan, the plumber, the cousin up from Casablanca, the guest who arrived an hour late. Saying no is barely an option. The first glass is the house saying you are welcome, and the second is the house meaning it.

The height has a purpose. The long fall cools the tea a little, carries the sugar through it, and throws air in so the top froths. A glass without foam looks tired, and a host who serves one will hear about it. The glasses are small, thick, often painted with gold, and too hot to hold anywhere but the rim.

It arrived later than you might think. Green tea came in by sea, through the Atlantic port of Essaouira, where merchants, many of them from the town's Jewish families, moved tea and sugar from European ships into the interior. For a long while it was a pleasure of the cities. After the Anglo-Moroccan treaty of 1856 ended the royal trade monopolies and set customs duties at ten per cent, tea imports roughly quadrupled in twenty years, and by the end of the century the pot had reached the countryside too. The leaves came from China then, and they still do. In 2025 Morocco bought more tea from China than any other country, around 280 million dollars of it.

The sugar matters as much as the leaves. In many houses it still comes as a loaf: a hard white cone of about two kilos, broken into chunks with a small hammer and kept in its own lidded box beside the tray. Two loaves go to a family when a young man comes to ask for their daughter's hand, and more are carried to weddings, to births, to neighbours home from the pilgrimage. Sweetness is how a Moroccan house says good news.

Then the three glasses. The leaves stay in the pot the whole time, so each round comes out different from the last, and there is a saying for it, told in several versions across North Africa: the first glass is as bitter as life, the second as strong as love, the third as soothing as death. Everybody stays for the third.

The pot changes with the year. In winter a sprig of wormwood, chiba, goes in beside the mint, bitter and grey-green, or a few leaves of louiza, lemon verbena, which also comes out on its own after dinner once the light has gone. That evening glass is the one we put in [[dusk]]. Mornings, in plenty of houses, belong to coffee with a pinch of cinnamon, ginger and pepper in the pot, which is where [[spiced-coffee]] comes from.

What tea really asks of you is time. You cannot gulp a glass that hot. You wait for it, you talk while you wait, someone brings out a saucer of almonds or [[bread-round-the-plate|a round of bread]] with a dish of oil, and the pot goes back on the flame for another round. An hour slips by. Nobody minds, and if you are lucky, lunch turns into [[the-second-helping|a second helping]] and then into more tea.

When the last glass is empty the host lifts the lid, looks in at the drowned mint, and reaches for the kettle.
