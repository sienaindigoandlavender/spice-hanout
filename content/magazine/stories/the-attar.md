The attar reaches behind him without looking. His hand knows the shelves the way a pianist knows the keys: dried rose buds on the left, cloves in the middle, a jar of cinnamon bark rolled tight as cigars, and above them a row of tiny glass vials with ground-glass stoppers, each one a different oil. A woman wants rose buds for the hammam and cloves for the pot. He weighs both on the same brass pan, tips them into two paper cones, and, because she has been coming for years, dabs a drop of something dark and sweet on the back of her wrist before she goes. She sniffs it all the way down the lane.

This is the attar's trade, and it has never seen a reason to split in two. The word comes from the Arabic ʿiṭr, perfume, which passed through Persian before it reached English as attar of roses. In Morocco the attar is the person who sells what smells good, and nobody has ever been able to draw the line between what smells good on the skin and what smells good in a tagine. Rose goes in both. So does clove, and amber, and orange blossom. The counter is one long argument that you should not have to choose.

In Fez the attars gave their name to the Souk al-Attarine, the market of the spice and perfume sellers, in the old heart of the city near the Qarawiyyin Mosque. They gave it to something grander too. Between 1323 and 1325 the Marinid sultan Abu Sa'id Uthman built a madrasa at the entrance to their market, and the people of Fez called it after its neighbours: al-Attarine, the madrasa of the perfumers. The courtyard is a box of tiles and lace. Zellige runs up from the floor in stars, carved plaster climbs above it in patterns too fine to follow, and higher still the cedar is carved and painted, warm brown against the white.

The students lived upstairs, in small spartan rooms, studying with the whole market drifting in at the windows. It is hard to think of a better address for a young scholar. Cardamom in the morning, cumin at noon, rose oil in the evening when the shops filled up again. Seven centuries later the madrasa takes visitors, and the smell still comes in from the lane.

Go into any attar's shop and the overlap is the first thing you notice. The rose buds sold for the bath are the same rose buds the cook crumbles into [[ras-el-hanout|ras el hanout]] when she wants it to taste like a feast day, and more than one merchant who mixes his own will tell you his secret is in that pinch of flowers. That blend is the attar's showpiece, [[head-of-the-shop|the best of the shop]] in one jar, and it reads like a list of his shelves. Rose from the valleys of the south, which we wrote about in [[rose]]. Clove, nutmeg, cinnamon. A little of everything that smells of somewhere far away.

The scent and the supper keep meeting after dark, too. Orange blossom is a perfume, a pastry water and a tea, all at once, and in [[dusk]] it sits beside lemon verbena and chamomile for the cup you drink after dinner, when the plates are cleared and nobody wants to get up yet.

We like the attar because he refuses to be practical. A spice shop that only sold what you need for dinner would be fine. His is better. You come in for cloves and you leave with a wrist that smells of roses, a twist of rose buds you had not planned to buy, and an idea for the pot that is entirely his fault.

At closing time he fits the stoppers back into the vials one by one, wipes the brass pan with his palm, and the last customer of the day walks out into the lane trailing amber and cumin behind her, like a dinner on its way home.
