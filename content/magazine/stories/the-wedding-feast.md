An aunt stands at the kitchen table with flour to her elbows, pinching almond paste into little crescents and lining them up on a tray that already holds eighty. Her sister is beside her doing the same. It is three weeks before the wedding and nobody in this family has bought a single biscuit. In Morocco it is the custom for relatives to band together long before the day, baking pastries and cooking ahead so that every guest who walks through the door will find something sweet waiting.

Those crescents are kaab el ghazal, gazelle horns: a thin dough wrapped round almonds, sugar and rosewater, curved like the horn they are named for. At a wedding they are a must, and they come with mint tea.

The guests take their time arriving. The card says eight, perhaps, and people drift in at nine, at ten, over a couple of hours, in their best kaftans. At the entrance someone holds out a dish of dates and a glass of milk, the old welcome of the house. Later in the night the bride and groom will give each other the same thing: a date, fed by hand, and a sip of milk.

Then the evening settles into its long middle. Juice comes round, and small tartlets, and tea poured high from the pot, glass after glass. Trays of sweets follow the tea, gazelle horns among them, and then more tea. The negafa, the woman hired to dress the bride and run the evening, appears and disappears with each change of kaftan. The music is loud. People dance. Children fall asleep on banquettes in their best clothes. And through all of it, the real food has not yet arrived.

That is the thing first-timers never quite believe. The main meal at a Moroccan wedding comes late, very late. One account from Rabat puts it at two in the morning. By then the room has danced through hunger and out the other side, and when the waiters finally come through the doors with the big dishes, a noise goes up that has nothing to do with the band.

Pastilla comes first. Every table gets its own, a wide round of paper-thin pastry baked golden, with chicken and almonds and spices inside and a dusting of icing sugar on top. Sweet and savoury in one bite, crackling at the edges, steaming in the middle. It is the dish people remember a wedding by, and the dish they argue about afterwards.

Then the chicken. Roasted chicken is so much a part of the night that it is sometimes simply called the wedding chicken, and it has been on the table for a very long time. Cookbooks from the Almohad era, written in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, already describe it, marinated with oil, vinegar, cumin, saffron, pepper, clove, cinnamon and ginger. Read that list again in a modern Marrakech kitchen and most of it is still on the shelf.

After the chicken, the meat. Lamb, ideally, brought to the table on a big platter dressed with vegetables, with olives and peppers and herbs on a side plate. In plenty of houses the meat for a family occasion is the sweet one, slow-cooked until it gives way, with prunes, honey and cinnamon and toasted sesame on top, the same dish we wrote down as [[lamb-and-prune-tagine|lamb and prunes]]. Everyone eats with bread, from the shared dish, with the right hand, the way [[why-we-eat-from-one-dish|a Moroccan table always eats]]. Somebody keeps pushing the best pieces towards you. You protest. They ignore you. This is [[the-second-helping]], at three in the morning, in sequins.

Then fruit, a whole platter of it, cold and bright after the meat. More tea. Sometimes ice cream, sometimes dates dipped in chocolate. By five the sky outside is turning grey, and the last guests go home at dawn with their shoes in their hands and a little parcel of gazelle horns wrapped in a napkin for the morning.

We made two blends that belong to nights like this. [[sweet-tagine]] carries the cinnamon and warm spice of the prune dish. [[kemoun]] is the cumin that goes on the table beside the meat, a pinch on the edge of the plate for anyone who wants it. Neither will get you to two in the morning on its own. The dancing does that.

In the kitchen the next day, the aunt counts what is left on the trays, which is almost nothing. She brushes the flour off the table with the side of her hand and puts the kettle on.
