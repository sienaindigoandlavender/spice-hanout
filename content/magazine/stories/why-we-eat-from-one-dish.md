The kettle comes round before the food does. A girl of about ten carries it in one hand and a brass basin in the other, and stops at each person in turn. You hold your hands out over the basin, she pours, you rub, she pours again, someone passes a towel. Then she moves on to the next chair, and the next, until everyone at the low round table has wet wrists and is looking at the kitchen door.

Ewers and basins like this have been part of the Moroccan meal for a long time. When an American couple, Talcott and Sophia Williams, travelled through Morocco in 1898, the objects they gathered ended up in the Penn Museum in Philadelphia. Among them is a heavy brass bowl with a pierced cover and two big loop handles, with a note from Mrs Williams: the kettle and basin are used to wash hands at meals. It is still in the museum's stores, waiting for someone to pour.

Then the tagine arrives and the lid comes off, and there is only one of it. One dish, set in the middle, and six people leaning in.

Nobody draws a line, but everybody can see it. The dish is a circle and the circle is divided, without a word, into slices like a cake. Your slice is the one that faces you. You tear a piece of bread, pinch it round a bit of sauce and vegetable from your own side, and eat. You do not wander. A hand that goes travelling across the dish towards the best of the olives gets noticed, and if it belongs to a child, gently returned.

That rule is older than any of the people at the table. In the hadith collected by al-Bukhari, a boy called Umar ibn Abi Salama, the Prophet's young stepson, is eating with him and reaching all over the dish. The Prophet tells him to say the name of God and eat from what is nearer to him. It is the kind of thing every grandmother in Morocco has said to a grandchild, in nearly those words, with the same patience. Another saying, in the collection of Ibn Majah, goes further: eat together and not separately, for the blessing is in being together.

So there is the name of God, softly, at the start. Bismillah. And there is the slice in front of you, which turns out to be a very good way to eat.

A shared dish sets one pace for the whole table. You cannot race ahead of everyone into a plate of your own, because there is no plate of your own. You eat at the speed of the person next to you, tearing bread, talking, reaching, pausing to listen to an uncle's long story about a car. When the sauce on your side runs low, someone tips the dish a little towards you without being asked. Everyone finishes at the same time, because everyone has been eating the same thing.

And there is the meat. In many houses it sits in the very middle of the tagine, under the vegetables, and nobody touches it while the rest of the dish is being eaten. The cooks of Morocco build a tagine in a mound for a reason. At the end, when the vegetables and the sauce are gone from the edges and the middle is all that is left, the meat is shared out, often by the host or the eldest at the table, who breaks it with her fingers and pushes the best pieces towards the guest, then the children, then everyone else. You said you were full. That was before the meat. A Peace Corps volunteer who lived with a Moroccan family wrote that eating this way made them feel closer to everyone around them, because you have to sit near enough to reach.

It is a very old idea of good manners, and it is also very greedy. Nobody is counting what you eat, because nobody can. The dish keeps no score. What it asks is that you stay at the table until it is finished, eat from your side, and take the piece someone else chose for you.

We keep two blends for this kind of dish. [[chermoula]] for a fish tagine on a weekday, the fillets laid over potatoes and peppers, with green herbs and oil rubbed in first. [[sweet-tagine]] for the slow ones, lamb under prunes and almonds, when the whole family is coming. Both are made for [[the-second-helping|the second helping]], and for [[bread-round-the-plate|bread wiped round the plate]].

When it is over the girl comes back with the kettle and the basin, the same way round the table. You hold out your hands. The water runs a little orange.
