The grandmother is the first to go. She pushes the last piece of bread round the empty dish, says something about her knees, and lowers herself onto the long banquette that runs along the wall of the salon. A cushion goes under her head, a blanket over her feet. Somebody half closes the shutters. In the kitchen the water for tea is already on, but nobody is in a hurry to drink it, because the house has entered the hour after lunch, and that hour belongs to no one.

Lunch is the big meal in Morocco, the one with the [[the-friday-couscous|couscous]] on Friday and the tagine on every other day, eaten slowly from one dish with the whole family round it. What comes after it is less written about and just as loved. The plates go back to the kitchen. The children are told to be quiet and are not. The men stretch out on the banquettes, the women claim the cushions, the cat takes the patch of sun by the door, and for an hour or so the house breathes very slowly.

There is an old word for this in Arabic, qaylula, the rest in the middle of the day. It turns up in the early collections of the Prophet's sayings and in stories of his companions. In one, gathered by the ninth-century scholar al-Bukhari, the caliph Umar walks past a group of men chatting in the heat of the day and tells them to get up and go and have their midday nap. Another remembers simply that people used to gather and then sleep at noon. In a hot country with a dawn prayer, the nap is good sense with a long pedigree.

It is also a quiet tug of war with the modern clock. Since 2005 Moroccan government offices have run a continuous working day, with a short break at lunch instead of the long one that let people go home to eat. In 2011 a minister told parliament that the pull of a tagine at noon and the siesta after it was hard to compete with. You cannot legislate a family meal out of existence, and plenty of people still drive home through the midday traffic for one, then sit a moment too long on the banquette before going back.

Anyone who has spent August in Seville or a Sunday afternoon at a grandparent's table in Lyon will know the feeling. The sun is at its highest, the food has done its work, and the body asks, politely, for nothing to happen. In much of the Mediterranean the answer has always been to listen. The shops in the old medinas pull their shutters down too, or the shopkeeper dozes upright on a stool with his radio murmuring, and the alleys go quiet in a way they never are in the morning.

Then the tea. When the house begins to stir, somebody lights the gas under the pot, and the smell of mint drifts through to the salon. [[tea-poured-from-a-height|The glasses go round]], small and very hot, and the talk starts up again where it stopped, a little softer than before. Someone brings out a saucer of almonds or a few dates. Nobody mentions the time. On the slowest days that glass turns into a second pot, and the afternoon is half gone before anyone notices.

In some houses the after-lunch glass is not mint at all. A few leaves of louiza, lemon verbena, go in the pot, or a pinch of chamomile, and the cup comes out pale gold and smelling of lemons and hay. That gentler pot is the one we built [[dusk]] around, even though in plenty of homes it belongs to the hour after lunch as much as to the evening. Our [[a-whole-day|box of three tins]] keeps that rhythm too: something bright for the morning, the big spices for lunch, and something soft for afterwards.

Nothing here needs earning. The [[the-second-helping|second helping]] came first, and the nap is simply what follows it. Outside, the light slides off the wall of the courtyard. Inside, the grandmother opens one eye, hears the glasses chiming on the tray, and asks whether anyone thought to make her one.
