By ten on a Friday morning the grain has already been through the steam once. A woman tips it out of the top of the pot into a wide wooden dish, sprinkles it with cold salted water, and works it between her palms until every lump falls apart. Then it goes back up over the broth. Then out again. Three times, in many kitchens, before anyone calls it ready.

The pot is a couscoussier, two storeys of it: a deep belly below where the broth simmers, a perforated basket above, the keskas, where the grain sits in the rising steam. The join between them is wrapped tight so nothing escapes. The steam has one job, and it is to go through the couscous.

Downstairs in the broth, the week's vegetables are taking their turns. The tough ones go in first, carrots and turnips and cabbage, then pumpkin and courgette, the soft ones last so they arrive whole. Seven is the number people like to say, and some families count. Others use what the market had on Thursday and nobody complains. A chicken or a piece of lamb sits underneath it all with chickpeas, onion, pepper, ginger, turmeric and a thread or two of [[saffron]]. The kitchen smells of it by eleven. The whole stairwell smells of it by half past.

Everything is timed backwards from one moment. The Friday midday prayer comes with a sermon, and the men come back from the mosque hungry and on the dot. The dish has to be on the table when the door opens. In plenty of houses the older women of the family have cooked together all morning, and the grown children have driven across town, because Friday lunch is the one meal of the week that pulls the whole family home. Missing it needs a very good excuse.

Couscous is so tied to this kind of gathering that when UNESCO added it to its list of the world's intangible heritage in 2020, on a joint file from Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Mauritania, the citation spoke as much about sharing and conviviality as about the grain. It covered the whole chain: the grain in the field, the semolina rolled by hand, the clay and wooden tools, the steaming, the eating. Knowledge passed on, it said, by watching and copying. Which is exactly how it happens in a Friday kitchen, with a teenager at the cook's elbow being told to put the spoon down and use her fingers.

Then the dish comes out. One wide gsaa, the couscous raised into a mountain, a well dug in the top for the meat, the vegetables laid down its sides in stripes of orange and green. Broth is ladled over until the grain shines, and more broth waits in a bowl for anyone who likes it wetter. Some families crown it with tfaya, onions and raisins cooked down slowly with [[cinnamon]] and a little sugar until they are sticky and dark. Often there is a jug of lben, cold buttermilk, to drink alongside.

Everyone eats from the side of the dish in front of them, many with their hands, rolling a little ball of grain and broth against the palm and flicking it into the mouth. Children learn early not to raid the middle. The best piece of meat travels across the mountain towards the guest, pushed by someone else's fingers, as at every [[the-second-helping|Moroccan table]]. A dish that took all morning can vanish in a quarter of an hour.

And often a little is set aside before anyone sits down: a plate on a tray, sometimes with fruit, carried next door to a neighbour who lives alone.

We grind [[ras-el-hanout]] for exactly this kind of pot. Two pinches stirred into the broth while the vegetables take their turns, and the steam carries it up through every grain above. For the sweet side, a spoon of [[sweet-tagine]] in the tfaya.

By two o'clock the gsaa is wiped clean with the last of the bread. Someone puts the kettle on. The grain left stuck to the keskas will wait until the evening, and somebody will pick at it, standing up, in the half-dark kitchen.
