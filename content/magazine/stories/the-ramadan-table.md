The soup goes on in the middle of the afternoon. A woman stands at the stove with the shutters half closed, stirring lentils and chickpeas into tomato, a little lamb at the bottom of the pot, a fistful of chopped parsley and coriander and celery leaves going in after. By four o'clock the smell has left the kitchen and gone out into the derb, where it meets the same smell coming out of every other kitchen on the street. Nobody has eaten since before sunrise. Nobody will until the sun goes down.

This is harira, and for one month a year it is on almost every table in Morocco at the same minute. One Moroccan told National Geographic it was ninety-nine per cent of the country, every evening of the month. Each family has its own way. Some colour it with turmeric, some with a thread of saffron. Most reach for ginger and cinnamon. Some add rice, some broken vermicelli. At the end comes the tadouira, flour stirred into water until it is smooth, poured into the pot in a slow ribbon while someone whisks, so the soup turns from broth to velvet in about ten minutes. The tomato sellers know what is coming: during Ramadan their prices go up, and by late afternoon the bunches of coriander can be hard to find.

The table is laid long before anyone can touch it. Dates in a small bowl. Hard-boiled eggs, with a saucer of salt and cumin beside them for dipping. Olives, lemon wedges, fresh cheese, honey, a stack of msemen and harsha. A jug of milk. And a plate piled high with chebakia, the dark twisted pastries that glisten as if they were varnished.

The chebakia were made weeks ago. In many neighbourhoods women gather in one house for several days before the month begins and make enough for all of it: flour and ground sesame, anise and cinnamon, a little mastic, orange blossom water, the dough cut and folded into its knot, fried, then dropped into honey and showered with sesame seeds. Every pastry shop in the country sells them too, by the kilo. Families who make them at home make too many on purpose, because a tin of chebakia is the thing you carry to a neighbour.

Then everybody waits. The children are sent to stand by the door. The tea is ready. The spoons, in old houses deep and carved from lemon or orange wood, are laid beside the bowls. The call to the sunset prayer rises from the mosque, and the whole street goes quiet for the length of one breath.

The first thing is a date. The Prophet broke his fast with dates and water, and many Moroccan families still begin the same way: a sip of water, a date, often three, an odd number. Then the harira, scalding, with a squeeze of lemon, and a chebakia between spoonfuls, so that the honey and the sour soup take turns. The silence lasts about as long as the first bowl. After that the noise comes back all at once: the clink of glasses, someone asking for the eggs, the television, a cousin arriving late with another tin of pastries.

The Moroccan word for this meal is ftour, breakfast, which is what it is. It is the first food since dawn. And it is only the beginning of the night. Later, often between ten and one in the morning, comes a proper dinner: a tagine, salads, fruit, more tea. Families visit, streets fill, cafés stay lit. In Ramadan the whole country eats at the sun's hours, and the clock on the wall stops mattering very much.

The last meal comes before dawn, and for that someone has to wake you. In the old medinas that job belongs to the nafar, a man in a jellaba and tarbouch who walks the dark alleys at around three in the morning blowing a long horn, a tune that is not the call to prayer and not quite music either. He works only in Ramadan and stops when Eid is declared. People thank him with sugar, with food, with a few coins. Phones and alarm clocks have pushed him out of the new neighbourhoods, but in the old city of Rabat you can still hear him, somewhere a few streets away, telling you there is time for bread and tea before the sky goes pale.

Then the month turns, and it turns again the next year a little earlier, because Ramadan follows the moon and slips back through the seasons. Some years the fast ends near nine on a long June evening. Some years it ends before six, in winter, with the soup pot steaming the windows.

The spices in that pot are the ones in [[golden-hour]]: turmeric, ginger, cinnamon. The saucer by the eggs is [[kemoun]]. The slow tagine that comes at midnight, lamb under prunes, wants [[sweet-tagine]]. None of it is complicated. All of it is about the moment the call to prayer stops and someone passes you the dates.

Somebody always finishes the chebakia. The plate comes back to the kitchen with a ring of honey on it and a few sesame seeds, and a child runs a finger round the edge.
