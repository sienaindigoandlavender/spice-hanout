The boy has the tray balanced on one hand and the cloth slipping off it with every step. Under the cloth are five round loaves, risen since breakfast, still soft enough to take a fingerprint. He turns left at the fountain, ducks under a beam, and follows the smell of woodsmoke down the derb to a low doorway with a black mouth of fire at the back. He hands the tray over without a word. He will be back in under an hour.

This is the ferran, the neighbourhood oven, and the old medinas of Marrakech and Fes have them in quarter after quarter, a short walk from the door. The dough is made at home: flour, water, salt, a little yeast, kneaded in a wide clay dish by a woman who has done it most mornings of her life. Then it goes out into the street, on a tray, on a board, on a child's head, to be baked over somebody else's fire. One fire, many households. Lighting an oven for five loaves would be a waste of wood, and in a medina summer no one wants that heat in the kitchen.

The man inside is the furnachi. He works from a pit in front of the oven, the floor at about the height of his chest, and moves everything with a long wooden paddle, sliding loaves in, turning them, drawing them out. He knows the hot corner and the gentle one, and how long each thing needs. Most of all he knows whose bread is whose. Some families mark their dough before it leaves the house. In one Fes ferran the baker dimples every loaf with a stick in a code only he can read. Plenty of bakers never need the marks at all. They know the shape of your mother's bread the way you know her handwriting.

The fire eats olive wood, farm scraps and shavings swept up from the carpenters' workshops. In many quarters the oven sits beside the hammam, sometimes sharing a wall, so the same heat works twice. The price has always been small. In Fes it was half a dirham a loaf, a coin you could spare every day, and a busy baker there can turn out three thousand loaves between morning and night.

Bread is only the start. Watch the doorway long enough and the whole neighbourhood's week goes past it. A tray of sesame biscuits. A fish tagine for lunch. A pastilla, wide as a cartwheel and fragile as paper, carried with two hands and a held breath, which tells the whole street that somebody has guests. If you are busy, you leave your tray on the step and a neighbour carries it down for you, pays the baker, and brings it back warm. Nobody keeps a ledger. It all comes out even in the end, give or take a loaf.

The bread comes home in time for the midday meal, wrapped in a cloth to keep it soft, and is torn at the table rather than cut. Half of it does the work of a fork, [[bread-round-the-plate|scooping and pinching]] at the tagine. The rest gets eaten for no reason at all. A heel dragged through olive oil and then through a saucer of [[moroccan-zaatar]], the [[wild-thyme]] catching on the oil. A warm quarter split open around a hard-boiled egg and a pinch of [[kemoun]]. A piece eaten standing up in the doorway, before lunch, because it is still hot and someone has to check.

There are fewer ferrans than there were. More women work outside the home now, and more families buy their bread from the corner shop. But the old ones are still lit every morning, and they still smell of what they always smelled of: woodsmoke, toasted crust, a little sesame burning on the oven floor.

At the end of the morning the boy comes back up the derb. The cloth is off. The loaves are dark gold, blistered at the edges, and one of them has a corner missing that will be blamed on the oven.
