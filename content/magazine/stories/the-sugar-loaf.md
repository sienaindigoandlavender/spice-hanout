The aunt holds the cone in the crook of her arm like a baby and taps it with the little hammer, once, twice, a third time harder. A chunk the size of a walnut breaks off and skitters across the tray. She drops it in the teapot, then breaks another for later, then a smaller one for the child who has been waiting by her knee with an open hand. The rest of the loaf goes back into its box with the lid on, a white mountain with a dent in its side.

Sugar in a cone looks old-fashioned anywhere else in the world. In Morocco it is simply sugar. It sits beside the tea tray in a lidded box of brass or wood, it is broken off as needed, and it does a job no packet of granules could do: it carries news. Families bring sugar loaves to weddings, to engagements, to births, to a neighbour home from the pilgrimage. They bring them to a house in mourning too, and set them down without a word. The same white cone says congratulations in one doorway and I am sorry in the next. Some arrive wrapped in a piece of cloth, and the cloth sometimes carries a smudge of henna from the last celebration it went to.

When a young man's family goes to ask for a daughter's hand, the sugar goes with them. It is the politest possible opening line. Nobody has to say we hope this ends sweetly. The loaf on the table has said it already.

The habit is older than the tea it sweetens. Four hundred years ago, sugar was one of Morocco's great fortunes. The Saadian sultans grew cane in the south, in the Souss valley around Taroudant and near Chichaoua on the road out of Marrakech, and pressed it in mills turned by water. A Spanish visitor of the time, Diego de Torres, counted several mills around Taroudant, and by the end of the sixteenth century there were eighteen working in the Souss. At Chichaoua the ruins of two factories still lie by the riverbed, built, the chronicles suggest, under Ahmad al-Mansur, the sultan who began the El Badi palace in Marrakech in 1578.

Morocco was then a serious exporter of sugar to Europe, and al-Mansur's wealth rode on it. The story told about the palace, which has the shape of a good legend, is that its Italian marble columns were paid for in sugar, weight for weight. Whether or not the scales were really that neat, the image sticks: a ship leaving for Italy low in the water with sugar, and coming back with stone.

It did not last. The troubles after al-Mansur's death in 1603 dragged on until around 1610, cheaper sugar from the Americas arrived, and the mills went quiet after barely half a century. The mill ruins are still there, and so is the craving. Sugar came back to Morocco as an import for a few hundred years, and in 1929 a refinery in Casablanca, Cosumar, started making it locally again. It kept the cone, because nobody would have bought it any other way.

What the cone gives you is a kind of ceremony at the scale of a kitchen. You do not measure. You break. A big piece for a pot of mint tea that has to stretch round a crowded room, a small one for a glass you are drinking alone at dusk, a sliver for the child. The hammer is heavy for its size and cold in the hand. The sugar is so hard it rings. In the hot tea it gives way slowly, a corner at a time.

The sweetness also crosses into the pot. In a kitchen that cooks lamb with prunes, the sugar and the spice come in together: cinnamon, a little ginger, a thread of saffron, honey or sugar caramelising in the last half hour until the sauce clings. That is the evening idea behind [[sweet-tagine]], the blend that lets a stew go sticky and dark at the edges. And in the morning, a chip off the loaf goes into coffee with a pinch of [[spiced-coffee]], so the first cup of the day is sweet before anything else is.

Nobody counts the lumps. A Moroccan host will drop sugar into your glass without asking, and if you say less, please, they will hear more. Sweetness is how the house says it is glad you came. You can read more of that at the [[tea-poured-from-a-height|tea tray]], or at the [[the-wedding-feast|wedding feast]], where the loaves pile up in the corner of the room.

By the end of the evening the cone in the box is shorter and lopsided, chipped on one side where the aunt always hits it. There are white crumbs on the tray and on the child's fingers. Tomorrow someone will open the lid, pick up the hammer, and start again.
