The woman behind the counter has her hands in a bowl of oil, and she is not sparing it. She pulls a ball of dough from the tray, presses it flat on the marble until you can almost read through it, scatters a little semolina over it and folds it into a square. Then she folds the square again, and flattens it again, and it goes onto the hot griddle beside six others already turning gold. Her shop is about the size of a wardrobe: a counter, a skillet, a stack of paper, one more woman at the back doing the same thing. A schoolboy hands over a coin or two and walks off with his msemen folded in paper, shiny with butter and honey, eating it before he reaches the corner.

Msemen is all the breakfast a hurried person needs. It is also, at home, the start of something much longer.

Because in a Moroccan house, breakfast arrives in pieces and nobody minds the wait. The tea goes on first, green tea and fresh mint and more sugar than anyone admits to, and it will be [[tea-poured-from-a-height|poured from a height]] and poured again before the morning is out. Bread comes next: yesterday's round of khobz warmed on the pan, or harcha, the thick round semolina bread, gritty and buttery, torn into wedges. A saucer of olive oil. A dish of black olives. A soft white cheese. Somebody's fried egg with the yolk still running, and a little pinch of salt and [[cumin]] on the side for dipping.

Then the baghrir, if it is a good day. Baghrir is the pancake of a thousand holes, a thin semolina batter that rises on the pan and covers itself in tiny craters as it cooks. It is only cooked on one side. The top stays pale and open, which is the whole point: you pour melted butter and honey over it, warm, and every one of those holes fills up. It is the kind of food you eat over the plate, fingers first, and then lick.

And in a small bowl beside it, if anyone in the family has roots in the south, there will be amlou. It comes from the Souss, the argan country around Agadir, where the trees grow wild almost nowhere else on earth. It has three things in it: almonds toasted until they smell of biscuits, local honey, and argan oil, the oil women crack out of the nuts by hand in the cooperatives of the region. Ground together, it turns into a loose, nutty, honeyed paste somewhere between peanut butter and praline. You spread it on msemen. You spoon it onto baghrir. You eat a bit straight from the spoon while nobody is looking.

Early in the morning, the street has its own breakfast. Carts and little counters ladle out bissara, a thick soup of dried fava beans blended smooth, with cumin and chilli on top and a ribbon of olive oil. A hunk of bread goes in with it. Men eat it standing, steam on their faces, before the shops open.

What ties all of it together is the clock, or rather the lack of one. A weekday breakfast can be quick when it has to be. A weekend breakfast, or a breakfast with guests, spreads across the table and stays there. More tea is made. Someone goes out for more msemen. The baghrir plate is wiped clean with the last of the bread, the way [[bread-round-the-plate|bread finishes every plate]] here. There is always room for the second glass, and the third.

We made two blends for that hour. [[golden-hour]] is the colour of the first sun on a white wall, turmeric and ginger and cinnamon, and it belongs in the oil under the morning eggs. [[spiced-coffee]] is for the people who would rather have coffee than tea, the way the old cafés of the medina make it, half a spoonful stirred in with the grounds before the water goes on. Neither is in any hurry.

By ten the griddle shop has sold through its stack and the woman is wiping down the marble with the flat of her hand. At home, the tea glasses are still on the table, a little honey has set in the bottom of the baghrir dish, and somebody is reaching for the amlou with the very last corner of msemen, one more time.
