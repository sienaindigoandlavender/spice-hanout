The man at the butcher's counter has already cut the beef shank into pieces, and the customer is filling the urn standing up, right there on the step. Meat first. Then garlic, whole cloves of it. A preserved lemon, quartered and pushed down the side. A heaped spoonful of cumin, then another, because one never looks like enough. A few threads of saffron, a good pour of olive oil, and from a twist of paper, a knob of smen, the aged butter that smells like a cheese cellar. No onions, no carrots, no tomatoes. A splash of water. He folds a square of paper over the mouth of the jar, ties it tight with string, tucks the whole thing under his arm and walks off down the street. The cooking took him four minutes. The waiting will take the rest of the day.

The jar is the tanjia, and so is the dish. It is a tall unglazed clay urn, round in the belly, with a neck so narrow you can only just get a hand inside. That neck is the whole trick. It holds the steam in, so the meat cooks in its own juices and fat, slowly, the way a French confit does, and nothing escapes but the smell.

Where he is going is round the back of the hammam. Every quarter of the Marrakech medina has a bathhouse, and every bathhouse has a fire under it heating the water for the steam rooms. Down a few stone steps, in a hot, low room, a man called the furnachi feeds that fire all day with wood, farm scraps and the shavings swept up from the carpenters' workshops. He takes the urn, finds a place for it at the edge of the furnace, and pushes it into the glowing ashes up to its shoulders. He might charge the price of a coffee. He does not need a label. He knows whose jar is whose.

Then the urn sits there for hours, four at the very least, overnight if you have the patience. The fire was going to burn anyway. The baths were always going to be hot. The tanjia just borrows the heat on its way past.

Ask in Marrakech why it is called the bachelors' dish and you will hear the same story with small changes. Kitchens at home were run by women. Tanjia was the men's own thing, made by the craftsmen of the souks, the leatherworkers and the metalworkers and the dyers, who filled a jar in the morning, dropped it at the furnace on the way to the workshop, and picked it up in the evening when the light went. No stove, no wife needed, no pot to wash. People also say that before the French came, when the weekend ran Friday to Saturday, the men of the city would carry their urns out to the gardens on their day off and eat together in the shade. Nobody can date it exactly, but it is old: a Marrakech scholar of the seventeenth century, al-Marghiti, already wrote of it.

It is still mostly men who make it, and still mostly men who argue about it. More cumin or less. Lamb or beef. Smen or no smen. Whether the lemon goes in whole or in quarters. Every one of them is right.

The good part is the opening. Back at the table, or on a blanket under the olive trees, the string comes off and the paper comes off and the steam comes up, sharp with lemon and dusty with cumin, saffron somewhere underneath. The meat has given up entirely. You do not use a fork. You tear a round of khobz, the bread from [[the-communal-oven|the neighbourhood oven]], and reach in with it, and the urn goes round the circle from hand to hand until someone tips it right up to get at the last of the sauce at the bottom.

Restaurants in the medina spice it harder now, with dried fruit and longer lists. The jar from the hammam keeps it short: [[cumin]] you can see, [[saffron]] you can smell. At home on a cold night, a heavy pot with a tight lid in a low oven is nobody's tanjia, but a spoonful of [[kemoun]] and a pinch of [[taliouine-saffron]] with the lemon and the garlic will get the kitchen most of the way there. A little [[ras-el-hanout]] if you want the restaurant version. Go to bed. Eat it tomorrow.

By evening the furnace room is lined with urns waiting to be collected, their paper lids gone brown and soft, string loosening a little in the heat. A man comes down the steps, points at one without a word, and carries it home still too hot to hold anywhere but by the neck.
