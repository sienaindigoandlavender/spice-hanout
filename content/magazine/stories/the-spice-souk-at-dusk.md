The spice seller has been sitting on the same stool since morning, and now, with the sun gone behind the roofs, he finally stands up. A woman in a house dress and slippers wants cumin. He sinks a tin scoop into the sack, tips it onto the brass pan of his scale, takes a pinch back off with his fingers, then adds it again because she is a regular. The cumin goes into a cone of paper, the top is folded down twice, and she is gone into the crowd before the scale has stopped swinging.

This is Rahba Kedima, the old square, a lopsided triangle of paving just east of Souk Semmarine in the Marrakech medina. In the daytime it belongs to visitors. Baskets hang in stacks, straw hats climb the walls, little bottles of perfume line up on shelves, and the stalls nearest the middle sell everything from knitted beanies to tea. Long before any of that, the square held auctions on set days of the week, wool here, carpets there, each trade with its own corner. Next door, a covered yard the French named the Criée Berbère, the Berber auction, is still the place for carpets.

At dusk the square changes hands. The tour groups have gone back to their riads to shower before dinner, and the people of the neighbourhood come out to shop for the evening. They know what they want and they buy it in small amounts: a hundred grams of ground cumin, a spoon of paprika, a fistful of fresh mint for the tea, a few threads of saffron folded into a scrap of plastic for a dish that matters. Nobody buys a kilo of anything. Spices go flat in a cupboard, and the stall is two minutes from home.

Walk a few steps north-west and you are in Souk el-Attarine, the souk of the attar. The word is old and lovely. An attar is the person who sells scent and spice together, from the Arabic for perfume, and it is the same word that came into English in the eighteenth century as attar of roses. In the stalls that still keep the old trade, the two have never been separated. Rose buds sit beside cloves, amber beside cinnamon bark, and a dusty jar of grey ghassoul clay, dug from Jbel Rhassoul near Missour in the Moulouya valley, waits for the hammam on Friday. In the old days the spice merchants had their own funduq nearby, an inn of rooms round a courtyard where men, mules and sacks could all spend the night.

The light goes fast in November, a little before six, and the souk turns on its bulbs one stall at a time. The colours change under them. Paprika looks redder, turmeric looks like a lamp itself, and the brown of [[ras-el-hanout|ras el hanout]] goes almost purple. Each seller has his own version of that blend, of course, and each will tell you the others are wrong, which is a whole [[head-of-the-shop|story of its own]].

What you notice most at this hour is the smell. All day it has been warm dust and leather. Now the cooking has started in the houses behind the walls, onions going soft in oil, a tagine of something with [[cumin]], and from the direction of Jemaa el-Fna, a few minutes' walk south, the first smoke of the food stalls setting up for the night. UNESCO describes that square as busy "all through the day, and well into the night", and the night half is the hungry half. Everyone in the souk is thinking about dinner, the sellers included.

So the last sales of the day are the generous ones. A seller rounds the weight up. Another throws in a sprig of mint. A boy runs a glass of tea across from the café, because the man at the stall has been talking for eleven hours and it is time. If you have nowhere to be, there is usually a stool, and someone will pour you [[tea-poured-from-a-height|a glass]] too.

Our blends started in streets like this one, with a scoop, a scale and a paper cone. [[kemoun|Kemoun]] is that cumin toasted and salted for the little dish on the table, and [[a-whole-day|A Whole Day]] is the souk's three moods in three tins.

By the time the call to the night prayer rolls over the roofs, half the shutters are down. The spice seller ties the neck of his cumin sack, sweeps the spilt powder off the counter into his palm, and tips it back in.
