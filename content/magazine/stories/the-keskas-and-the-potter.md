The woman at the market stall lifts the clay basket up to the light and looks through it, the way you would hold a colander up to check for cracks. The sun comes through the holes in its floor in a little constellation. She taps the rim with a fingernail, listens, taps again, and nods. It rings, so it goes home with her, wrapped in yesterday's newspaper and carried on top of the vegetables.

That basket is a keskas, the top half of a couscous pot, the part with the holes. Underneath it, on the stove, sits a belly of broth. On top, the grain takes the steam. The [[the-friday-couscous|Friday couscous]] depends on it, and for most of history it was made by a potter, out of the same earth as the water jars and the tagines, shaped, pierced while the clay was still soft, and fired until it rang.

When UNESCO put couscous on its list of the world's intangible heritage in 2020, on a joint file from Algeria, Mauritania, Morocco and Tunisia, the citation did something charming. It did not stop at the grain or the cook. It said the dish comes with "a set of exclusive tools, instruments and utensils", and then it named who makes them: clay utensils by potters, wooden ones by cooperatives and artisanal workshops, often family-owned. Somewhere in that sentence is a man at a wheel and a woman at a lathe, and a couscous that would not happen without them.

There is a keskas in Philadelphia that proves how long this has been going on. The Penn Museum holds a small clay strainer bowl brought back from Morocco by the Williams expedition in 1898. It is brown, burnt from use, fourteen and a half centimetres across, with a pierced base and three little lugs standing up from the rim. The museum's note says it was used for "coos coos, the national dish", the meat in the pot below and the grain cooked in the strainer above. Somebody's lunch, every week, for years, and then a trip across the Atlantic in a crate.

Then there is the dish the couscous ends up in. At Harvard there is an eighteenth-century bowl from Fez, red earthenware a foot across, glazed and painted inside with a pomegranate in blue, yellow and green. The museum calls it a mokhfia, the kind of vessel used to serve couscous. Pile the grain high in the middle with the vegetables leaning against it, and as the family eats their way down from all sides, the pomegranate slowly comes back into view. Dessert, of a sort, for the eyes.

Between the pot and the serving bowl comes the wide dish where the work is done, the gsaa. Wood or clay, broad and shallow, it is where the steamed grain is tipped out, sprinkled with water, raked through with the fingers and loosened before it goes back up over the broth. A good one is passed down. Its surface goes silky with years of palms and butter and argument about who rolls best. Nobody in the house remembers buying it, which is the highest compliment you can pay a kitchen tool.

Why does a dish need its own pot at all? Because a cook who loves couscous wants it steamed, slowly, through a floor of small holes, the grain swelling a little each time it goes up, and clay is patient with heat. The tool is the recipe, in a way. The potter decided, long ago, how fast the steam rises and how each grain feels on the tongue.

Plenty of kitchens use a metal couscoussier now, and nobody blames them: metal does not crack when you put it down too hard. But clay ones are still made, and still sold in the markets, and some cooks swear they can taste the difference, a faint earthiness in the grain, which may be true or may be love.

By Friday the new keskas is sitting over the broth with a ring of steam escaping round its rim, the grain inside going plump, a spoon of [[ras-el-hanout]] already stirred into the pot below. When the grain is tipped into the dish and the family [[why-we-eat-from-one-dish|leans in from all sides]], nobody thanks the potter. They just eat, and go back for more, and the clay basket sits cooling on the counter with its little holes full of light.
