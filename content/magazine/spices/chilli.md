At the end of a long lunch, someone pushes a saucer of red paste towards the cousin who likes it hot, and the rest of the family carries on eating as before. In many Moroccan homes that is where the fire lives: in a small dish at the side, for whoever wants it, so the tagine itself can stay gentle for the grandmother and the children.

The chilli in that saucer, *felfla harra* in Darija, the hot pepper, is a newcomer by the clock of this kitchen. Peppers were domesticated in what is now Mexico some six thousand years ago. Columbus tasted one on Hispaniola, took it for a cousin of the black pepper he had sailed off to find, and the mistake gave us the name. Chillies reached Europe in 1494 as garden curiosities, and Portuguese sailors, who liked them as a cheap stand-in for pepper, carried them round the world from there.

The heat is a trick aimed at mammals. In a study published in Nature in 2001, researchers from the University of Florida and Northern Arizona University found that birds feel nothing from capsaicin, the molecule that burns, and pass the seeds whole and ready to sprout, while rodents, which chew seeds to pieces, leave the fruit alone. The plant was warning us off all along, and we kept coming back for seconds.

Further east the paste took the starring role. In 2022 UNESCO added Tunisia's harissa to its list of intangible heritage: peppers dried in the sun, split, seeded and ground with garlic, coriander and salt, usually by women working together at family or neighbourhood gatherings, then packed into glass or clay jars.

Our [[dry-harissa]] keeps that shape and leaves the oil to you: sun-dried chilli, smoked [[paprika]], garlic, caraway and [[coriander-seed]]. A little more chilli sits quietly inside [[chermoula]], under the cumin and the lemon. Stir a spoon of the harissa into olive oil, set it at the edge of the table, and watch who reaches for it first, and who reaches again.
