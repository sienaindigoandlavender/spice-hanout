The onions are already soft in the oil when the cook reaches for the little jars. Coriander, white pepper, turmeric, a few threads of saffron, and a good spoonful of pale brown powder that smells of warm wood and lemon peel. The chicken goes in, then the olives and the preserved lemon, and the lid comes down for the afternoon.

That powder is ginger, *skinjbir* in Darija, and in a Moroccan kitchen it is almost always dried and ground. Fresh ginger is juicy and sharp, the kind that bites. Dried, it loses the juice and keeps the heat, which comes out slower and rounder, closer to a hearth than a spark. It does not shout in a tagine. You only notice it when it is missing and the sauce tastes oddly flat.

It is everywhere once you look. It warms the broth of the [[the-friday-couscous|Friday couscous]], it goes into harira with cinnamon and turmeric when the pots come out at sunset in Ramadan, and it is one of the steady names on the long list inside a ras el hanout.

It came a long way to get here. Ginger was first grown in maritime Southeast Asia, carried by Austronesian sailors, then by traders through India to the Mediterranean by around the first century. The Romans paid dearly for it. In fourteenth-century England a pound of ginger cost about as much as a whole sheep, which tells you something about how much people wanted it.

It sits next to [[turmeric]] in our [[golden-hour]] for the first cup of the day, beside cinnamon and saffron in [[sweet-tagine]], and deep in our [[ras-el-hanout]].
