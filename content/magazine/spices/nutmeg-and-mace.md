The spice seller picks a nutmeg out of the sack, shakes it by his ear to show it is a good one, and drops it into your palm. It is heavier than it looks, brown and wrinkled like a walnut that has been out in the sun. At home you take the small side of the grater to it, and after a few strokes the whole kitchen smells warm and a little like a Christmas pudding.

It comes from very far away. The tree grows wild in the Banda Islands, a handful of volcanic dots in the Moluccas of eastern Indonesia. Harvesters pull the fruit down with a wicker basket on a long bamboo pole. Slice away the outer shell and inside sits the seed, wrapped in a saffron-red net that clings to it like lace. Peel that net off and dry it, and you have mace. Dry the seed for about a week and a half, until the nut rattles in its shell, and you have nutmeg. Two spices from one fruit.

Arab merchants ran the trade through the Middle Ages, carrying it to China and to Europe, and nutmeg had found its way into the kitchens of the Abbasid caliphs a thousand years ago. Later the Dutch wanted the Bandas so badly that at the Treaty of Breda in 1667, England gave up its claim to Run, a speck of a nutmeg island, and the Dutch gave up theirs to a wooded island across the Atlantic called Manhattan.

In a Moroccan kitchen nutmeg is the quiet one. It hides in the slow, sweet dishes, under the [[cinnamon]] in a [[lamb-and-prune-tagine|tagine of lamb and prunes]], and most spice sellers put a little in their [[head-of-the-shop|ras el hanout]]. Mace is softer and more floral, the one you notice only when it is gone.

We grate nutmeg into [[sweet-tagine]] and a touch into the [[spiced-coffee]], and our [[ras-el-hanout]] carries both, nut and lace together, the way they grew.
