At first light a woman walks the edge of a barley field with a sack slung from her shoulder, snapping off pink flowers with quick fingers. The bushes grow along the borders of the plots, and she works fast, because the sun is coming. Once it is high and hot the petals lose their scent, so the picking starts at dawn and is over in about six hours. By then a good picker has filled her sack many times over, some twenty kilos in a day, and carried it on her head to the weighing station.

This is the Damask rose, *Rosa damascena*, in the valley around Kelaat M'Gouna, between the High Atlas and the Sahara, where two rivers water a strip of green through the stony south. The story told in the valley is that the rose came with the caravans, long ago, and stayed. It flowers for a few weeks in April and May, and in 2025 the agricultural office in Ouarzazate expected 4,800 tonnes of it. The town does not hide its devotion. There are rose statues at the entrances, the taxis are pink, and every May the International Rose Festival fills the streets. The 2025 edition was the sixtieth.

Most of the harvest goes into copper stills. It takes four to five tonnes of flowers to make a single kilo of rose oil, and in 2021 a kilo of that oil sold for around eighteen thousand dollars. The rose water is the gentler cousin, sold by the bottle in every grocer's in the country. Some of the buds are simply dried, and those are the ones that end up in the spice shops, piled in baskets beside the cloves, faded to a dusty pink and still smelling of May.

In the kitchen a crumbled bud does something no other spice can. It lifts the warm, dark things around it, cinnamon and nutmeg and pepper, and leaves a faint sweetness at the back of the mouth. We grind dried petals into the end of our [[ras-el-hanout]], the way the old blends had them, and into [[sweet-tagine]], where they sit beside [[cinnamon]] and wait for the prunes.

Open the tin and breathe in before you cook. Somewhere under the cumin there is a field at dawn.
