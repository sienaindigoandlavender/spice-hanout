The cook has two jars of pepper on the shelf above the stove, and she reaches past the black one. The chicken is already in the pot with grated onion, oil, a spoon of [[ginger]], a spoon of [[turmeric]] and a few threads of saffron soaked in warm water. The sauce is going to be gold, glossy, with green olives and slices of preserved lemon floating in it at the end. She wants it smooth all the way through, no black specks, so in goes the pale pepper, a good pinch, and the lid goes on.

White pepper is not a different plant. It is the same vine as [[black-pepper]], the same Malabar climber, only the berries are left to ripen longer and then treated quite differently. Black pepper is picked as the spikes start to turn and dried whole, skin and all. For white, growers take the ripe berries and sink them in water, traditionally in a river or a still pond, for two or three weeks. The outer skin softens and starts to rot, and then it is rubbed away, leaving the small cream-coloured seed inside, which is washed and dried.

What is left tastes cleaner and quieter at first, then warm, a heat that arrives late and stays at the back of the mouth. Some people find it a little earthy from all that soaking. In a pot of chicken and lemon it simply disappears into the sauce and leaves the heat behind.

Indonesia is one of the great white pepper growers, behind China and Vietnam. In Moroccan kitchens it turns up in the golden chicken dish called *djaj mqualli*: the version Saveur printed asks for two teaspoons of it beside ginger, turmeric, coriander and saffron.

We keep to black pepper in our own jars. It warms the [[golden-hour]] milk and sits among the spices in our [[ras-el-hanout]]. White pepper lives on the shelf beside them, for the days when the sauce should shine like brass and taste of something you can't quite see.
