The spice seller tips two small heaps side by side on a sheet of paper, both brown, both ridged, both shaped like a grain of rice with a slight bend in it. One is [[cumin]], one is caraway, and the customer leans in close, sniffs, and laughs, because from a metre away nobody can tell. Up close it is easy. Cumin smells of the grill and of warm skin. Caraway smells cooler and sweeter, a little of aniseed, a little of rye bread, with a clean, almost minty lift at the end.

The confusion is old. The Greek word *karon*, which Merriam-Webster gives as the root of caraway, seems to have meant cumin too. The word travelled into Arabic as *karawya*, into medieval Latin as *carvi*, and reached English some time in the Middle Ages. In German the two still share a name: caraway is *Kümmel*, and cumin is *Kreuzkümmel*, more or less "cross-caraway", as if one were the other's cousin from the next village. Which, in a way, it is. Both belong to the carrot family, along with [[coriander-seed]], fennel and dill.

What we call the seed is really the plant's dried fruit, about three millimetres long, with five pale ridges running down its back. The flavour sits in its oil, mostly a compound called carvone, and that is the cool lift you notice on the tongue. Spearmint carries its mirror-image twin, the same molecule turned the other way round, and the two do not smell the same.

Northern Europe leads the trade now: the Netherlands, Poland and Germany grow the most, and Finland ships a good share of what is sold abroad. There it goes into rye loaves, sauerkraut and aquavit. But it has long been known in the Arab world, and grown in Morocco too.

In our [[dry-harissa]] it sits beside sun-dried [[chilli]], smoked paprika, garlic and coriander. It is not there to be noticed. Stir a spoonful into olive oil, let it stand ten minutes, and wipe it up with bread. The chilli arrives first, then the garlic, and then, just as the heat settles, a faint sweet coolness at the back of the mouth, and you reach for the bread again to find out what it was.
