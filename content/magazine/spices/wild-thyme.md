A herb seller in the souk lifts a dry grey-green bundle off the pile, rubs it between his palms over the scale, and the whole stall smells of hot hillside. Ask what it is and he will say *zaatar*, which tells you the family and very little else.

In Morocco the word covers a handful of wild plants rather than one. In the Middle Atlas and the Rif it usually means *Origanum compactum*, a small oregano of these mountains and the leading herb in a good number of traditional dishes. Further south it can mean *ziitra*, a thyme with leaves like savory that grows only in Morocco and Algeria, from about 350 metres up to 2,200, on stony pasture, in scrub and in the clearings of forests. It flowers in May and June, and most of it is still picked wild.

Plenty of people want it. Moroccan exports of this one thyme climbed from about 1,051 tonnes in 2002 to 2,777 tonnes in 2014, and botanists in the High Atlas now worry about hillsides cut too often and plants pulled up by the root. A herb that has always been free for the gathering is finding out what it is worth.

At home it goes two ways. A sprig dropped into the teapot in winter, beside or instead of the mint, turns the [[tea-poured-from-a-height|glass of tea]] resinous and warm, the way a pine wood smells after rain. And crumbled over bread with olive oil, it becomes the kind of thing you keep eating long after you meant to stop, the oil running down your wrist, a last crust kept back to [[bread-round-the-plate|wipe the plate]].

Our [[moroccan-zaatar]] starts with the mountain thyme and adds toasted sesame, sumac and a little salt, the sprinkle the whole Mediterranean shares. Pour a saucer of oil, tip the jar over it, tear the bread.
