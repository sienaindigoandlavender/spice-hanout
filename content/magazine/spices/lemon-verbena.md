The plates have gone back to the kitchen and someone's aunt is still picking at the last of the bread when the teapot comes out again. This time there is no mint in it. A handful of long, narrow leaves goes in, the water goes over, and by the time the glasses are filled the room smells like a lemon that has been left in the sun.

In Darija the leaf is *louiza*. Field surveys in the south record it as *lwiza*, the leaf taken as an infusion, which is exactly how it turns up at the end of a long evening. The name gives its story away. The plant comes from South America, from Chile, Peru and Argentina, and the Spanish carried it to Europe in the seventeenth century. In 1819 botanists named its genus *Aloysia* for María Luisa of Parma, wife of King Carlos IV of Spain, and in Spain it is still *hierba luisa*, Luisa's herb. Somewhere along the way a queen's name crossed the strait and settled into a Moroccan teapot.

Rub a leaf between finger and thumb and it feels a little rough, almost papery, and leaves your hand smelling for an hour. The scent is citral, the same pair of aromatic molecules that gives lemon peel and lemongrass their brightness. On the shrub the leaves grow in whorls of three, and in high summer it puts out loose sprays of tiny white and pale lilac flowers. Morocco grows it for its oil too, alongside France and Algeria.

It is a herb for the end of things. After the afternoon's [[tea-poured-from-a-height|mint tea]] and the wintry sprig of [[wild-thyme|zaatar]], louiza is the soft one, pale gold in the glass, happy with a spoon of honey and a long conversation that goes nowhere in particular.

Our [[dusk]] starts there: lemon verbena, with chamomile, orange blossom and a little fennel seed. Make a pot, carry it to the sofa, and let the evening take as long as it likes.
