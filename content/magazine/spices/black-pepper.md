The cook tips a spoon of turmeric into the oil, then a spoon of ginger, and then, without measuring, a hard twist of black pepper straight from the jar. Three spices, one after another, the way her hands have done it a thousand times. Ask her which one matters most and she will shrug. The pot just tastes wrong without the pepper.

*Lebzar*, as Darija calls it, comes from very far away. The vine is native to the Malabar Coast of India, a woody climber that pulls itself ten metres up a tree by its little aerial roots. The berries hang in long spikes. When the first ones start to redden, the whole spike is picked and the fruit is dried in the sun until it turns black and hard. White pepper is the same berry with its dark outer skin softened in water and rubbed away, which is why it tastes cleaner and quieter.

People have been greedy for it for a very long time. In the Roman cookbook that goes under the name of Apicius, 349 of its 468 recipes call for pepper. When Alaric's Goths besieged Rome in 408, the Senate sent out wagons of gold and silver, 4,000 silk tunics and 3,000 pounds of pepper to make them go away. In the Middle Ages, Venice and Genoa held the European trade so tightly that sailors went looking for a sea route to India just to get round them.

One Moroccan saw it growing with his own eyes. Ibn Battuta reached the Malabar Coast in the fourteenth century and wrote that the pepper vines were planted beside the coconut palms and climbed them the way grapevines climb.

Here it is the spice nobody notices until it is missing. It warms the [[the-friday-couscous|Friday couscous]], it sits beside [[ginger]] and [[turmeric]] under a chicken tagine, and it is on the list of every [[head-of-the-shop|ras el hanout]]. We put it in [[golden-hour]] for warm milk at dawn, in our [[ras-el-hanout]], and a little in the [[spiced-coffee]], where it catches at the back of the throat after the cinnamon has gone soft.
