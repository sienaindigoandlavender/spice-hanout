In the fields around Oulad Ali Loued, on the plain below Beni Mellal, a farmer walks between peppers spread on the bare ground and turns them over with his hands, one long red stripe after another. It is autumn in Tadla. From late summer into the winter the flat land here goes red, and the air smells faintly sweet and dusty, like a spice jar left open in the sun.

These are the peppers that become paprika, *tahmira* in Darija, the powder that makes a Moroccan sauce red. Here the pepper itself is called *niora*. Tadla grows most of the country's crop, and Oulad Ali has its biggest market for it. It takes about six kilos of fresh peppers to make a single kilo of powder, so a pile you could lie down in grinds to a sack you could carry.

The name gives away the route. Peppers came from the Americas, where they were first grown in what is now Mexico thousands of years ago. In Spain, at the end of the fifteenth century, Hieronymite friars at the monastery of Guadalupe began growing them, passed them to their brothers at Yuste, and from there to a convent of the same order at La Ñora, near Murcia. *Ñora*, *niora*. Murcia's peppers are sun-dried much the way Tadla's are. Further north in La Vera, where autumn rain makes sun-drying impossible, farmers dry theirs for two weeks over smouldering oak, which is where smoked paprika gets its campfire taste.

In the kitchen, tahmira does the quiet work. It turns [[chermoula]] the colour of brick and gives fried fish its red edge, it makes kefta taste of more than meat, it reddens the oil in a pot of lentils until you want bread to chase it. It is sweet and mild, and it is impatient: thrown into oil that is too hot it scorches in seconds and goes bitter, so it goes in with the onions and the garlic and takes its time with them.

Sweet paprika is the first red in our [[chermoula]]. Smoked paprika sits beside sun-dried chilli in our [[dry-harissa]], for the fire you add at the table, with a pinch of [[cumin]] never far away.
