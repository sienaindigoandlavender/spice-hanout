On a flat roof in the medina, a woman lays strips of beef over a line in the sun, one after another, like washing. The night before she rubbed every piece with garlic, cumin and crushed coriander seed. In a few days the meat will be dark and leathery, cooked slowly in oil and fat, and packed into a jar under more fat. This is *khlii*, and when it finally meets a pan of eggs, the whole kitchen smells of it.

The coriander is the part you notice last. Cumin shouts. Coriander seed stays behind it, round and warm, with a brightness that the Encyclopaedia Britannica describes as something like lemon peel and sage. Crack one between your teeth and you get it at once: citrus, a little nutty, a little floral.

It is one of the oldest spices anyone has found. Remains of coriander seed turned up in the tomb of Tutankhamun, and Pliny the Elder thought the best coriander came from Egypt. The Romans used it to flavour bread. Later it went into sausages, liqueurs and the sugar-coated seeds the English called comfits.

In Morocco the same word, *kasbour*, covers both the seed and the green leaves that come from it, and a spice seller will ask which you mean. The leaves go fresh into tagines by the fistful. The seed goes into the grinder.

Toast it for a minute in a dry pan and it smells of orange peel. Grind it and it softens the edges of everything around it. It is the quiet half of our [[chermoula]], next to paprika and [[cumin]]. It sits under the cinnamon and pepper in [[ras-el-hanout]]. In our [[dry-harissa]] it rounds off the chilli and caraway so the heat lands warm and slow, with bread to chase it.
