Behind the counter of an old café, a man splits a green pod with his thumbnail and tips the black seeds into the coffee before the water goes in. Nobody watching could tell you the proportions. By the time the pot comes to the table the whole room smells of it, sweet and cool at once, like eucalyptus that grew up in a bakery.

That pod is cardamom, *qaqula* in the medina, and it has come a very long way to get here. The plant is native to the evergreen forests of the Western Ghats in Kerala, in southern India, and the Indian Institute of Spices Research calls it the queen of spices. It is a leafy thing taller than a person, yet the flowers grow on shoots that trail along the ground, so the pods are picked low, almost from the forest floor. Each pale green capsule, under two centimetres long and paper thin, holds fifteen to twenty seeds. They are picked before they fully ripen, for the best flavour.

Arab sailors and caravan traders were carrying cardamom from India to the Middle East some two thousand years before Europeans reached the Americas. It remains the third dearest spice in the world, after saffron and vanilla. Today the biggest crop grows in Guatemala, the leading producer, where German settlers brought the seed, and where the Inter-American Development Bank counts around 350,000 families who live from it. In 1995 about sixty percent of Guatemala's cardamom went to Saudi Arabia, to flavour the coffee.

In Morocco it goes into the pot too. Our [[spiced-coffee]] puts it beside cinnamon, ginger and nutmeg. It is one of the spices a seller adds to [[ras-el-hanout]] when he wants to show off. In [[golden-hour]] a little of it sits on top of the turmeric and pepper, warm milk underneath, honey on the spoon.

Crack a pod near your nose before it goes in. Then pour the coffee, and when the cup is empty, pour again.
